Abstract:  
19 
Animals have exquisite control of their bodies, allowing them to perform a diverse range of 
20 
behaviors. How such control is implemented by the brain, however, remains unclear. Advancing 
21 
our understanding requires models that can relate principles of control to the structure of neural 
22 
activity in behaving animals. To facilitate this, we built a ‘virtual rodent’, in which an artificial 
23 
neural network actuates a biomechanically realistic model of the rat 1 in a physics simulator 2. We 
24 
used deep reinforcement learning 3–5  to train the virtual agent to imitate the behavior of freely-
25 
moving rats, thus allowing us to compare neural activity recorded in real rats to the network 
26 
activity of a virtual rodent mimicking their behavior. We found that neural activity in the 
27 
sensorimotor striatum and motor cortex was better predicted by the virtual rodent’s network 
28 
activity than by any features of the real rat’s movements, consistent with both regions 
29 
implementing inverse dynamics 6. Furthermore, the network’s latent variability predicted the 
30 
structure of neural variability across behaviors and afforded robustness in a way consistent with 
31 
the minimal intervention principle of optimal feedback control 7. These results demonstrate how 
32 
physical simulation of biomechanically realistic virtual animals can help interpret the structure of 
33 
neural activity across behavior and relate it to theoretical principles of motor control.

Main Text:  
36 
Humans and animals control their bodies with an ease and efficiency that has been difficult to 
37 
emulate in engineered systems. This lack of computational analogues has hindered progress in 
38

--- PAGE 3 ---

 
motor neuroscience, as neural activity in the motor system is only rarely interpreted relative to 
39 
models that causally generate complex, naturalistic movement 8–11. In lieu of such generative 
40 
models, neuroscientists have tried to infer motor system function by relating neural activity in 
41 
relevant brain areas to measurable features of movement, such as the kinematics and dynamics of 
42 
different body parts 12–15. This is problematic because movement features are inherently correlated 
43 
through physics, and the representational models based on them can only describe behavior, not 
44 
causally generate it 8,16. Here, we propose an alternative approach: to infer computational 
45 
principles of biological motor control by relating neural activity in motor regions to models that 
46 
implement their hypothesized functions and replicate the movements and behaviors of real animals 
47 
(Fig. 1 A-B).  
48 
 
49 
To enable this line of inquiry and probe its utility, we developed a virtual rodent by training 
50 
artificial neural networks (ANNs) controlling a biomechanically realistic model of the rat to 
51 
reproduce natural behaviors of real rats. This allowed us to relate neural activity recorded from 
52 
real animals to the network activity of the virtual rodent performing the same behaviors. Analogous 
53 
approaches have proven successful in relating the structure of neural activity to computational 
54 
functions in other domains, including vision 17–19, audition 20, olfaction 21,22, thermosensation 23, 
55 
perceptual discrimination 24, facial perception 25, and navigation 26,27. However, there have been 
56 
relatively few attempts to similarly model the neural control of movement, and those that did, 
57 
mainly probed how artificial controllers resemble neural activity during specific motor tasks or 
58 
across a limited range of behaviors or effectors. Regardless, these pioneering efforts demonstrated 
59 
the capacity of simple brain-inspired controllers to reproduce animal locomotion 10,28,29, showed 
60 
how biomechanics can influence neural representations of movement 9, and revealed similarities 
61 
between the representations of movement in artificial and biological neural networks 8,30,31.  
62 
 
63 
Modeling the neural control of diverse, natural behaviors is a larger undertaking with several 
64 
unique challenges. Importantly, because animals evolved to skillfully control their bodies to solve 
65 
challenges in complex environments 32, our models should control biomechanically realistic bodies 
66 
in closed-loop with physically realistic environments 7. Furthermore, since animals express a 
67 
diverse range of species-typical behaviors, our models should be able to replicate these 33. Finally, 
68 
our models should demonstrate robustness to neural noise and other sources of variability inherent 
69 
to biological control systems 7,34. Modeling the neural control of movement at this degree of 
70 
richness and realism has been hampered by a scarcity of high-fidelity 3D kinematic measurements, 
71 
tools to physically simulate animal bodies, and methods to build agents that replicate the diversity 
72 
of animal behavior.  
73 
 
74 
To overcome these challenges, we developed a processing pipeline called MIMIC (Motor 
75 
IMItation and Control) (Fig. 1 C-F, Supplementary Video 1). MIMIC leverages 3D animal pose 
76 
estimation 35 and an actuatable skeletal model 1 amenable to simulation in MuJoCo 2, a physics 
77 
engine, to build a virtual rodent that can imitate natural behavior under realistic constraints. 
78 
Specifically, MIMIC uses deep reinforcement learning 3,4 to train ANNs to implement an inverse 
79 
dynamics model, a function which specifies the actions (i.e. joint torques) required to achieve a 
80 
desired state (i.e. body configuration) given the current state. We used the ANNs to control a 
81 
biomechanical model of the rat, training it to imitate the movements of real rats across their 
82 
behavioral repertoire. This allowed us to directly compare neural activity in freely moving animals 
83 
to the activations of inverse dynamics models enacting the same behaviors.  
84

85 
We used this approach to interpret neural activity in the sensorimotor striatum (dorsolateral 
86 
striatum in rodents, DLS) and motor cortex (MC) of rats, two hierarchically distinct structures of 
87 
the mammalian motor system for which the neural representation of natural behaviors have 
88 
previously been described 36–39. We found that the structure of neural activity across behaviors was 
89 
better predicted by the virtual rodent's network activity than any kinematic or dynamic feature of 
90 
movement in the recorded rat, consistent with a role for both regions in implementing inverse 
91 
dynamics. Furthermore, by perturbing the network’s latent variability, we found that it structures 
92 
action variability to achieve robust control across a diverse repertoire of behavior in a manner 
93 
consistent with theoretical principles of optimal feedback control7. Furthermore, the network 
94 
activity was predictive of the structure of neural variability across behaviors, suggesting that the 
95 
brain structures variability in accordance with these principles.

Results: 
99 
To compare an artificial control system to a real brain producing natural behaviors requires 
100 
measuring the full-body kinematics and neural activity of real animals. To this end, we recorded 
101 
the behavior of freely-moving rats in a circular arena with an array of six cameras while measuring 
102 
neural activity from the DLS or MC (DLS: 3 animals, 353.5 hours, 1249 neurons; MC: 3 animals, 
103 
253.5 hours, 843 neurons) with custom 128-channel tetrode drives (Fig. 1 C, Extended Data Fig. 
104 
1). To infer full-body kinematics from the videos, we tracked the 3D position of 23 anatomical 
105 
landmarks (keypoints) on the animal using DANNCE 35 (Fig. 1 D, Extended Data Fig. 2 A-C, 
106 
Supplementary Video 2). We used a feature extraction, embedding, and clustering approach to 
107 
identify discrete behaviors from kinematic data, as described previously 40–43. To enable physical 
108 
simulation in MuJoCo 2, we registered a skeletal model of the rat 1 with 74 degrees-of-freedom 
109 
(38 controllable degrees-of-freedom) to the keypoints using a custom implementation of the 
110 
simultaneous tracking and calibration (STAC) 44 algorithm (Fig. 1 E, Extended Data Fig. 2 D-F, 
111 
Supplementary Video 3). We next compiled a diverse catalog of behavioral motifs (847 5-second 
112 
snippets) spanning the behavioral repertoire of the rat to provide training data for our ANN 
113 
controllers.  
114 
 
115 
Controlling a complex body to perform diverse natural behaviors requires a remarkable degree of 
116 
flexibility in the underlying control system. Biological control systems are widely believed to 
117 
achieve such flexibility by implementing internal models, i.e., neural computations that 
118 
approximate the complex dynamics of the body. Here, we focus on the simplest feedback 
119 
controller that uses an internal model to recapitulate the behavioral repertoire of the rat (see 
120 
Supplementary Discussion 1). This minimal controller takes as inputs the current state of the body, 
121 
its desired future state, and uses an internal model called an inverse dynamics model to estimate 
122 
the action required to achieve the desired future state given the current state 4,6. Despite its 
123 
relatively simple formulation, building a single controller that replicates diverse behaviors while 
124 
controlling a complex body is a challenging task for which performant methods have only recently 
125 
been developed 3–5,45.  
126 
 
127 
Therefore, to build virtual rodents that imitate real animal behavior, we trained ANNs to 
128 
implement inverse dynamics models using deep reinforcement learning as in recent work (Fig. 1F) 
129 
3,4. The networks accepted as input a reference trajectory of the real animal’s future movements 
130 
ACCELERATED ARTICLE PREVIEW


--- PAGE 5 ---

 
and combined a compressed representation of the reference trajectory with the current state of the 
131 
body to generate an action, thus implementing an inverse dynamics model (Fig. 2 A). For ease of 
132 
discussion, we refer to the subnetwork that encodes the reference trajectory as the ‘encoder’, and 
133 
the remainder of the network as the ‘decoder’. The state vector was defined as the joint angular 
134 
position and velocity of the virtual rodent’s full-body pose, as well as simulated measurements 
135 
from inertial and force sensors. The reference trajectory was defined as the states (excluding the 
136 
inertial and force sensors) visited by the real rat in the immediate future (ranging from 20-200 ms), 
137 
expressed relative to the current state of the virtual rodent’s body. The action was defined as 
138 
torques at 38 actuators (joints) along the body. The networks operated over short timescales to 
139 
generate actions that moved the virtual rodent in the simulated environment, running at 50 Hz in a 
140 
sliding-window fashion to imitate arbitrarily long bouts of behavior. To study how different 
141 
network architectures and hyperparameters impacted imitation performance, we varied the decoder 
142 
architecture, regularization of the latent encoding, presence of autoregression, definition of the 
143 
action, and reference trajectory duration. During training, the states visited by the virtual rodents 
144 
were compared to the reference trajectory of the animal being imitated. This allowed us to calculate 
145 
the reward at each frame using multiple objectives related to different kinematic and dynamic 
146 
features of movement (see methods). Through trial-and-error, the networks learned to produce 
147 
actions that moved the body of the virtual rodent in ways that matched the real animal’s movements 
148 
(Supplementary Video 1).  
149 
 
150 
Controlling a high degree-of-freedom body to imitate diverse animal movements is a challenging 
151 
task for which the performance and generalization of artificial agents has only recently been 
152 
characterized 3–5,45. Remarkably, not only did the virtual rodent reliably and faithfully replicate 
153 
movements in the training set, but the ANN controllers also generalized to held-out movements 
154 
(Fig. 2 B-E, Extended Data Fig. 3). This success in imitating unseen examples allowed us to 
155 
evaluate the virtual rodent over the entirety of our dataset. To do so efficiently, we divided the 
156 
607-hour dataset into contiguous 50 second chunks, and ran the networks over all chunks in 
157 
parallel. We found that all trained networks were capable of faithful imitation, but networks with 
158 
recurrent decoders outperformed other architectures (Extended Data Fig. 3 A, B), particularly 
159 
during slower movements (Extended Data Fig. 3 C, D). For these networks, most of the deviations 
160 
from the real rat’s kinematics could be attributed to accumulation of error in the center of mass 
161 
over time (Fig. 2 D-F). To mitigate this, we implemented a termination and reset condition that 
162 
was triggered when the virtual rodents deviated excessively from the reference trajectory (see 
163 
methods). We used this to derive a measure of imitation robustness 46 by analyzing the distribution 
164 
of durations between resets, which we refer to as episode durations. Regardless of the specific 
165 
ANN implementation, the virtual rodent showed remarkable robustness, imitating long bouts of 
166 
behavior without termination (Fig. 2 G, Extended Data Fig. 3 B). Given the short timescale nature 
167 
of the inverse dynamics models, we wondered whether providing the networks with more context 
168 
about the upcoming movements would result in more robust control. For the most performant 
169 
architectures, increasing the length of the reference trajectory resulted in models with greater 
170 
robustness at the expense of imitation performance (Extended Data Fig. 3 G-I), suggesting a 
171 
tradeoff between robustness and imitation fidelity when selecting the duration of the reference 
172 
trajectory.  
173 
 
174 
Having models that faithfully imitate natural behaviors of real rats allowed us to compare neural 
175 
activity in real animals to the activations of a virtual rodent performing the same behaviors (Fig. 3 
176

A). To compare the dynamics of real and virtual control systems, we performed encoding analysis 
177 
and representational similarity analysis, established methods that allowed us to probe the 
178 
correspondences both at the levels of single neuron activity and population activity structure. To 
179 
establish a baseline and a point of reference, we estimated the extent to which measurable or 
180 
inferable features of behavior (representational models) relating to the kinematics and dynamics 
181 
of movement (Fig. 1B) could predict the activity of putative single-units (20 ms bins) in held-out 
182 
data using Poisson generalized linear models (GLMs). Consistent with previous reports 36,39,47, the 
183 
most predictive representational feature was pose (Fig. 3 B-C), with the activity of individual 
184 
neurons being best predicted by the kinematics of different body parts (Extended Data Fig. 4 A-
185 
D).  
186 
 
187 
We next compared the predictivity of the inverse dynamics models against representational 
188 
models. While we focus on a network drawn from the most performant class of models, namely 
189 
those with recurrent decoders, we note that all architectures exhibited qualitatively similar results. 
190 
Because the virtual rodent produced behaviors that deviated slightly from those of real rats (Fig. 2 
191 
D-E), our inverse dynamics model started at a disadvantage relative to the representational models, 
192 
which were referenced to the real rat’s movements. Despite this handicap, we found that the 
193 
inverse dynamics model predicted the activity in both brain regions significantly better than any 
194 
representational model, with the best results coming from the first layer of the decoder (Fig. 3 B-
195 
D, Extended Data Fig. 4 E-H, Extended Data Fig. 10). We observed similar results across striatal 
196 
cell types (see methods) (Extended Data Fig. 5). To estimate the temporal relationships between 
197 
neural activity, kinematics, and our inverse dynamics model, we trained GLMs using different 
198 
temporal offsets between the predictors and neural activity. Most neurons in DLS and MC were 
199 
premotor, meaning that their neural activity was best predicted by future kinematics and concurrent 
200 
activations of the inverse dynamics model (Extended Data Fig. 6).   
201 
 
202 
To analyze the structure of population activity in MC and DLS across behaviors and assess the 
203 
degree to which it is captured by representational models and inverse dynamics models, we 
204 
performed representational similarity analysis (RSA) 48. For our purposes, this involved 
205 
quantifying how different model features were structured across behaviors using a representational 
206 
dissimilarity matrix (RDM) and comparing the RDMs generated from representational features or 
207 
activations of the inverse dynamics models with those generated from neural population activity 
208 
in DLS and MC. For all features, we computed RDMs by calculating the average vector for each 
209 
behavior and computing the pairwise distance between these vectors using the cross-validated 
210 
Mahalanobis distance (see methods). While we focused on the most performant network, we note 
211 
that all networks exhibited qualitatively similar results. Individual neurons in DLS and MC were 
212 
preferentially tuned to specific behavioral categories, resulting in RDMs that reflect the population 
213 
activity structure across behaviors (Fig. 4 A, B). We found that the neural population activity 
214 
RDMs of both DLS and MC were more similar to the inverse dynamics model RDMs than those 
215 
of the representational models (Fig. 4 C-E). Moreover, when comparing across networks, we found 
216 
that the similarity between RDMs constructed from inverse dynamics models and neural activity 
217 
in DLS and MC was strongly correlated with the imitation performance and robustness of the 
218 
network (Fig. 4 F-I). This suggests that more performant models exhibit representations more 
219 
similar to those of both DLS and MC, consistent with previous reports comparing neural activity 
220 
with task-optimized neural networks 17,20. 
221 
 
222

To verify that the increased predictivity of inverse dynamics models relative to representational 
223 
models was a result of learning the dynamics of a realistic body, we changed the body to see if it 
224 
affected the fidelity of behavioral imitation and neural predictivity of our models. In a ‘mass 
225 
scaling’ experiment, we trained the virtual rodent to control bodies with total masses that varied 
226 
from half to twice the standard mass. In a ‘relative head scaling’ experiment, we trained it to 
227 
control bodies where the mass of the head relative to the rest of the body varied from half to twice 
228 
the standard ratio, while maintaining the same total mass. These subtle modifications to the body 
229 
model frequently resulted in policies with degraded imitation performance (Extended Data Fig. 7 
230 
A, B; Extended Data Fig. 8 A, B). They also reduced overall putative single-unit predictivity from 
231 
features of many inverse dynamics models (Extended Data Fig. 7 C, D; Extended Data Fig. 8 C, 
232 
D) and occasionally reduced the representational similarity to neural activity (Extended Data Fig. 
233 
7 E, F; Extended Data Fig. 8 E, F). These results show that subtle changes to the body model can 
234 
affect both the virtual rodent’s behavior and neural predictivity.  
235 
 
236 
We next studied how the predictivity of our inverse dynamics models compared to that of ANNs 
237 
implementing other control functions. To test this, we used data from the most performant inverse 
238 
dynamics model (see methods) to train ANNs via supervised learning 49,50 to implement a forward 
239 
model and a sequential forecasting model (Extended Data Fig. 9 A-C). Neither model could predict 
240 
putative single-unit activity in MC or DLS more accurately than the inverse dynamics model 
241 
(Extended Data Fig. 10 A-B). Similarly, neither model could predict the representational similarity 
242 
structure of MC and DLS as well as the inverse dynamics model (Extended Data Fig. 9 D-F), 
243 
consistent with these brain areas reflecting computations associated with inverse dynamics.  
244 
 
245 
In addition to imitating animal behavior and predicting the structure of neural activity, simulated 
246 
controllers allow us to study control processes that are difficult to access experimentally. A long-
247 
standing question that can be uniquely studied in this way relates to how movement variability is 
248 
shaped by the nervous system. It has been widely observed that animals structure movement 
249 
variability differently depending on the task, with variability preferentially quenched along task-
250 
relevant dimensions in accordance with the minimal intervention principle 7,51,52. In the context of 
251 
optimal feedback control, such ‘structured variability’ is thought to result from regularizations of 
252 
the cost functions associated with movement generation 52, such as the minimization of jerk 53 or 
253 
energy expenditure. However, with the notable exception of signal-dependent noise 54, how neural 
254 
activity in biological control networks shapes variability in motor output remains largely 
255 
unexplored (though see 55). To address this, we leveraged the stochastic nature of our inverse 
256 
dynamics models to study whether and how its ‘neural’ variability structured action variability.  
257 
 
258 
To probe the relationship between ‘neural’ variability and action variability, we focused on two 
259 
components of the network: the latent variability, a part of the latent encoding that parametrizes 
260 
the variability of a 60-dimensional Gaussian distribution, and the action the network outputs. We 
261 
used the generative nature of the latent encoding to relate latent variability at a given time point 
262 
(‘instantaneous’ latent variability) to the variability of the distribution of actions that emerge from 
263 
repeated resampling of this latent encoding (‘instantaneous’ action variability; Fig. 5 A). We use 
264 
the phrase instantaneous variability to differentiate from other types of variability, such as trial-to-
265 
trial variability or temporal variability. Importantly, these quantities can only be directly accessed 
266 
through simulation. 
267 
 
268

--- PAGE 8 ---

 
To determine whether the virtual rodent’s actions exhibited structured variability, we estimated 
269 
the instantaneous action variability at each timepoint (Fig. 5 A), and averaged across behavioral 
270 
categories. As in biological controllers, the structure of variability across the model’s actuators 
271 
showed a strong dependence on the behavior (i.e., the task) being performed (Fig. 5 B). However, 
272 
unlike in biological controllers, signal-dependent noise cannot contribute to this structured 
273 
variability as none of the sources of variability in the network were signal-dependent by 
274 
construction. Consistent with action variability being controlled by the network’s latent variability, 
275 
we found that their dissimilarity structure across behaviors were similar (Fig. 5 C), with individual 
276 
latent dimensions expanding or contracting their variability as the virtual rodent performed 
277 
different behaviors (Fig. 5 D). Indeed, the behavioral dependence was so strong that the latent 
278 
variability alone was sufficient to identify the behavior enacted at any given time (Fig. 5 E). To 
279 
relate the latent variability structure across behavior in the virtual rodent with the neural variability 
280 
structure of real animals, we compared RDMs derived from the latent variability and the temporal 
281 
variability of neural activity evaluated over a one second moving window (See methods). 
282 
Intriguingly, the latent variability structure resembled that of the temporal variability of neural 
283 
activity across behaviors (Extended Data Fig. 9 G-I), suggesting that the inverse dynamics models 
284 
predict not only the structure of neural activity but also its variability.  
285 
 
286 
To determine whether the structure of the virtual rodent’s latent variability afforded robustness in 
287 
accordance with the minimal intervention principle, we changed it in two different ways. We made 
288 
the variability across all dimensions of the latent encoding uniform and, in a different simulation, 
289 
inverted the variability, i.e., quenching it in dimensions with normally high variability and vice 
290 
versa (see methods) (Fig. 5 F). These deviations from the virtual rodent’s learned variability 
291 
structure resulted in poorer imitation and more frequent failures at equal noise levels (Fig. 5  F-H), 
292 
consistent with the system’s variability structure obeying the minimal intervention principle 7. To 
293 
be clear, we do not suggest that the latent variability itself improves performance or robustness. In 
294 
fact, models with stronger latent regularization, and thus greater latent variability, performed 
295 
slightly worse in terms of imitation reward and robustness on the testing set (Extended Data Fig. 
296 
3 E-F). Instead, these results, coupled with the training objective (see Supplementary Discussion 
297 
2), show that the virtual rodent adaptively shapes latent variability to increase robustness according 
298 
to behavioral demands (Fig. 5 I), affording robustness in the face of unquenchable noise. This 
299 
structured variability emerges solely from latent variable compression, suggesting a link between 
300 
mechanisms for robustness and generalizability (see also 25,56).

Discussion: 
303 
How the brain achieves robust and flexible control of complex bodies has remained a puzzle in 
304 
large measure due to the lack of expressivity in our models and their reductionist nature. Here, we 
305 
address these limitations by taking a holistic approach to sensorimotor control that emphasizes 
306 
embodiment, sensory feedback, stochastic control, and diverse behavior. Our approach reflects a 
307 
belief that motor system function cannot be understood independent of the body it evolved to 
308 
control or the behaviors it evolved to produce (Fig. 1A).  
309 
 
310 
To demonstrate the utility of this approach, we developed a virtual rodent in which a fully 
311 
configurable and transparent ANN controls a biomechanically realistic model of a rat in a physics 
312 
simulator (See Supplementary Discussion 3). In constructing such a system, we needed to balance 
313 
tractability, expressivity, and biological realism, both at the level of the biomechanical plant and 
314 
ACCELERATED ARTICLE PREVIEW


--- PAGE 9 ---

 
its controller. In this work, we opted for the simplest model that could recapitulate the behavioral 
315 
repertoire of the rat and predict the structure of neural activity in the brain across behavior. The 
316 
result was a plant with point-torque actuation and a controller implementing inverse dynamics. 
317 
Our simulations show that this level of model abstraction, which notably omits muscular actuation, 
318 
is already sufficient to achieve our objectives. As we and others extend these biomechanical 
319 
models to include whole-body musculoskeletal actuation, it will be interesting to probe the degree 
320 
to which such increased biomechanical realism further informs our understanding of the neural 
321 
control of movement. 
322 
 
323 
To train the virtual rodents to replicate spontaneous behaviors of real rats (Fig. 2), we developed 
324 
an imitation learning pipeline, MIMIC (Fig. 1 C-F). Remarkably, we found that the virtual rodents 
325 
generalized to unseen movements across the entirety of our dataset with high fidelity and 
326 
robustness (Fig. 2, Extended Data Fig. 3). In comparing the network activations to neural 
327 
recordings in real rats performing the same behaviors, we found that our model explained the 
328 
structure of recorded neural activity across a wide range of naturalistic behaviors better than any 
329 
traditional representational model (Figs. 3-4). Because the virtual rodent’s ANN implements an 
330 
inverse dynamics model, the observation that its network activations predict single-unit and 
331 
population neural activity in DLS and MC more accurately than measurable features of movement 
332 
or alternative control functions is consistent with these regions taking part in implementing inverse 
333 
dynamics.  
334 
 
335 
We believe that the improvements in predictivity relative to representational models result from 
336 
incorporating bodily dynamics, including the influences of gravity, friction, inertia, and 
337 
interactions between body parts. Note however, that one should ostensibly be able to find a 
338 
combination of measurable representational features that predicts neural activity as well as our 
339 
models. In fact, this is what our models do. They learn a nonlinear function, parametrized by a 
340 
neural network, that transforms the kinematics of desired future movements into the dynamics 
341 
required to achieve those kinematics. The network does this by encoding the physical realities of 
342 
bodily control in its weights; i.e., it learns an inverse dynamics model. There are at least two major 
343 
advantages of our approach relative to approaches based on representation. First, the models we 
344 
train are causal - they are sufficient to physically reproduce the behavior of interest as opposed to 
345 
merely describing it. Second, they place the emphasis on identifying the functions that brain 
346 
regions implement as opposed to merely describing the flow of information.   
347 
 
348 
Previous work in brain-machine interfaces 57 and oculomotor control 58 has similarly related neural 
349 
activity in the motor system to inverse dynamics models. Our work extends these findings to the 
350 
domain of full-body control and across a diverse behavioral repertoire. We note that a neural code 
351 
consistent with inverse dynamics could reflect and support other processes, including motor 
352 
learning 59 or even different control functions. However, in our experiments, models trained to 
353 
implement forward dynamics and sequential forecasting did not fare as well in predicting neural 
354 
activity structure (Extended Data Fig. 9 A-F), although we note that these controls may differ from 
355 
internal models (e.g. state estimation, forward dynamics models, etc.) implemented in a composite 
356 
controller 60. In future work, a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between 
357 
control functions and neural activity structure could be achieved by comparing the network 
358 
activations of such composite controllers to neural recordings in brain regions believed to 
359 
implement different internal models.

361 
While noise is inherent to biological control, how the nervous system deals with it to ensure 
362 
robustness and flexibility remains unclear 61. The minimal intervention principle speaks to this, 
363 
explaining that controllers quench movement variability along dimensions relevant to performance 
364 
7,51. By leveraging stochastic ANN controllers (Fig. 2 A), the virtual rodent allowed us to study 
365 
the relationship between network variability and variability in motor output. Intriguingly, we found 
366 
that the virtual rodent ‘brain’ regulated its latent variability to control action variability in 
367 
accordance with the minimal intervention principle 7 (Fig. 5). The structure of variability emerged 
368 
from training the network to balance latent variable compression, implemented to support 
369 
generalization, and the ability to faithfully imitate over the training set (Supplementary Discussion 
370 
2). Thus,  managing the trade-off between latent variable compression and motor performance 25,56 
371 
may structure neural variability in ways that are distinct from previously hypothesized mechanisms 
372 
like signal-dependent noise 54 or energy constraints 7. Together, these results reveal a link between 
373 
a computational mechanism for generalization (latent variable compression) and one for 
374 
robustness in control (structured variability). That the virtual rodent’s latent variability predicts the 
375 
structure of neural variability across behaviors in DLS and MC (Extended Data Fig. 9 G-I) further 
376 
suggests that the brain may structure neural variability in accordance with these principles.  
377 
 
378 
More generally, our results demonstrate how artificial controllers actuating biomechanically 
379 
realistic models of animals can help uncover the computational principles implemented in neural 
380 
circuits controlling complex behavior. We believe the potential of this approach is significant and 
381 
untapped. Virtual animals trained to behave like their real counterparts could provide a platform 
382 
for virtual neuroscience to model how neural activity and behavior are influenced by variables like 
383 
feedback delays, latent variability, and body morphology that would otherwise be difficult or 
384 
impossible to experimentally deduce. Inverse dynamics models trained to reproduce diverse and 
385 
realistic behaviors could also be reused as low-level modules to promote naturalistic movement in 
386 
neural networks trained to autonomously perform tasks, including those common in neuroscience 
387 
research 3,4. Similarly, since the ANNs controlling the virtual rodent are fully configurable, future 
388 
iterations could aim to implement brain-inspired network architectures to improve performance 
389 
and interpretability and probe the roles of specific circuit motifs and neural mechanisms in 
390 
behaviorally relevant computations.

641 
Skeletal model: We previously developed a skeletal model of a rat that matches the bone lengths 
642 
and mass distribution of Long Evans rats 1. The model has 74 degrees of freedom (DoF) and 
643 
defines parent-child relationships between body parts through an acyclic tree that starts with the 
644 
root (similar to the center of mass) and branches to the extremities. The pose of the model consists 
645 
of 3 Cartesian dimensions specifying the position of the root in space, 4 dimensions specifying the 
646 
quaternion that captures the orientation of the model relative to the Cartesian reference, and 67 
647 
dimensions that specify the orientations of child body parts relative to their parent’s reference 
648 
frame. The model has 38 controllable actuators that apply torques to specific joints. To help imitate 
649 
rearing, we increased the range of motion of the ankle and toe joints to [-.1, 2.0] and [-.7, .87] 
650 
radians. The model is equipped with a series of sensors, including 1) a velocimeter, 2) an 
651 
accelerometer, 3) a gyroscope, 4) and force, torque, and touch sensors on its end effectors. 
652 
 
653 
Skeletal registration: We used a custom implementation of STAC 44 to register the skeletal model 
654 
to the DANNCE keypoints. Briefly, STAC uses an iterative optimization algorithm to learn a set 
655 
of 3D offsets that relate different sites along the skeletal model to DANNCE keypoints (m-phase), 
656 
as well as the pose of the model that best reflects the keypoints at each frame given the set of 
657 
offsets (q-phase). To ensure consistent relationships between keypoints and model sites across 
658 
different poses, the offsets corresponding to keypoints closest to a body part were expressed in the 
659 
reference frame of the parent body part. 
660 
 
661 
In the m-phase, we optimize the offsets using L-BFGS-B over a dataset of 500 frames to minimize 
662 
the mean squared error between the true keypoints and fictive keypoints derived from applying the 
663 
offsets to the posed model. In the q-phase we optimize the pose of the model using least-squares 
664 
ACCELERATED ARTICLE PREVIEW


--- PAGE 17 ---

 
optimization over the same set of frames to minimize the same objective while keeping the offsets 
665 
fixed. At each step of the pose optimization, we reposition the model and compute new positions 
666 
of the fictive offsets via forward kinematics in MuJoCo.  
667 
 
668 
As the dataset totaled 607 hours of data sampled at 50 Hz, the registration algorithm needed to be 
669 
efficient. To speed up the q-phase, we separately optimized the pose of different body parts rather 
670 
than optimizing over the full body pose. First, we initialize the model’s root position as the position 
671 
of the middle spine keypoint and optimize only the 7 DoF specifying the Cartesian position and 
672 
quaternion of its root. We next optimize the quaternions of the root, trunk, and head to match 
673 
keypoints along the head and trunk of the animal. Finally, we individually optimize each limb. In 
674 
subsequent frames, we initialize the model’s pose using its pose in the previous frame. 
675 
 
676 
For each animal, we independently estimated the offsets using the procedure described above. We 
677 
accounted for differences in animal size by isometrically scaling the model by a scaling factor 
678 
manually determined via a visual comparison of models overlain on images of the rats. We found 
679 
this procedure to be more robust, faster, and produce comparable results to a direct optimization 
680 
of the scaling factor when learning offsets. In practice, we found that running the iterative 
681 
optimization three times produced reasonable offsets that could be used to estimate the skeletal 
682 
pose in new keypoint data.  
683 
 
684 
To infer the skeletal pose of an entire recording session, we ran the q-phase optimization a final 
685 
time using the set of offsets learned during training. To improve inference speed, we divided the 
686 
session into contiguous 20 second chunks and ran the q-phase optimization in parallel on Harvard 
687 
Research Computing’s Cannon HPC.

Behavioral segmentation: We automatically identified stereotyped behaviors throughout our 
690 
recording sessions using an unsupervised feature extraction and clustering approach described 
691 
previously 40–43. We extracted a high-dimensional feature vector capturing the multiscale dynamics 
692 
of the animal’s keypoints over time. The vector was composed of three types of features. The first 
693 
was the height of the keypoints from the floor, smoothed using a 60-ms median filter. The second 
694 
was the keypoint velocities, estimated using the finite differences method on smoothed (100-ms, 
695 
median filter) keypoint trajectories. The third was a multiscale time-frequency decomposition of 
696 
the rat’s pose, obtained by computing a pairwise distance matrix between keypoints for all frames 
697 
in the smoothed keypoint trajectories, decomposing the matrix into its top 20 principal 
698 
components, and applying a continuous wavelet transform to each principal component with 
699 
frequencies ranging from 0.5 to 20 hz.  
700 
 
701 
To aid in identifying diverse stereotyped behaviors, we implemented a sampling and clustering 
702 
procedure. For each session, we subsampled the feature vector by a factor of 20 and embedded it 
703 
into a 2-dimensional space using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding. We next clustered 
704 
the resulting space using hierarchical watershed clustering, and uniformly sampled 500 samples 
705 
across the clusters. Samples from each session were compiled into a single set and clustered to 
706 
automatically assign behavioral categories to individual frames using k-means clustering (K=100). 
707 
The resulting cluster centroids were then used to classify the remaining frames in the original 
708 
dataset.

Spike sorting: For each animal, the raw neural data from all sessions was sorted using an improved 
711 
implementation of Fast Automated Spike Tracker (FAST) 62. While the majority of the sorting 
712 
process remains unchanged between the implementations, there are three relevant modifications.  
713 
 
714 
Feature extraction: We applied a 𝛽-distribution (𝛽=100) weighting transform to the spike 
715 
waveforms to more heavily weigh the values near the spike peak. We next spectrally decomposed 
716 
the waveforms using a discrete wavelet transform with a Symlets 2 wavelet. Finally, we applied 
717 
Principal Components Analysis on the wavelet coefficients, retaining only the first 10 components. 
718 
 
719 
Clustering: We identified putative single units using ISO-split 63 rather than superparamagnetic 
720 
clustering.  We used an iso-cut threshold of .9, a minimum cluster size of 8, 200 initial clusters, 
721 
and 500 iterations per pass.   
722 
 
723 
Linking: To sort our long-term recordings, we clustered the feature-transformed data spanning 
724 
chunks of approximately 1 hour using ISO-split, and linked clusters across chunks using a variation 
725 
of the segmentation fusion algorithm detailed in FAST. The relevant modification was using the 
726 
Komolgorov-Smirnov criterion from ISO-split to link similar neurons across recording sessions. 
727 
 
728 
Criteria for unit selection: After manual curation, we used several summary statistics to further 
729 
assess the quality of putative single units. For encoding analyses, we excluded units with an 
730 
isolation index 62 less than .1, and a proportion of interspike interval violations greater than .02. 
731 
For single-unit analyses, we excluded units with average firing rates less than .25 Hz and a total 
732 
recorded duration that failed to span the entirety of the session in which they were measured. For 
733 
population analyses, we excluded units with average firing rates less than .05 Hz and those with 
734 
total recorded durations that failed to span the entirety of the session in which they were measured 
735 
(2092 putative single units, 1249 DLS, 843 MC). For all analyses, spike times were binned into 
736 
20 ms bins.

Imitation task: We used an imitation task similar to previous works on motion capture tracking 
756 
5,64,65, and most closely resembling CoMic 3. The task has four major considerations: initialization, 
757 
observations, reward function, and termination condition.  
758 
 
759 
Initialization: Episodes were initiated by randomly selecting a starting frame from the set of all 
760 
frames across all clips, excluding the last ten frames from each clip. The pose of the rat model was 
761 
initialized to the reference pose in the selected frame.

Observations: The model received as input a combination of proprioceptive information, motion 
764

765

766

771

Model inference  
903 
 
904 
Rollout: To evaluate the models on new data, we used the postural trajectories obtained from 
905 
STAC as reference trajectories. At each frame, the model would accept its current state and the 
906 
reference trajectory for the following frames and generate an action. After applying forward 
907 
kinematics, this action would result in the state at the next frame, closing the sensorimotor loop. 
908 
In the initial frame, the model’s state was initialized to the state of the real animal. For the encoding 
909 
and representational similarity analyses, we disabled the noise at the action periphery and the 
910 
sampling noise in the stochastic latent space. For analyses of the model’s latent variability, these 
911 
sources of noise remained enabled.  
912 
 
 
913 
At each frame, we recorded physical parameters related to the model’s state, activations of several 
914 
layers of the ANN controllers, and the fictive reward. The physical parameters included STAC-
915 
estimated keypoints, quaternion forces experienced at all joints, quaternion positions, velocities, 
916 
and accelerations, and the cartesian positions of all joints. The recorded ANN layers included the 
917 
latent mean and log standard deviation, the latent sample, all LSTM hidden and cell states, and the 
918 
action. 
919 
 
920 
To aid in comparing the network’s activity to neural activity, we maintained the termination 
921 
condition employed during training. This decision had two effects. First, it maintained that the 
922 
model’s behavior was within a reasonable range of the true behavior. Second, it ensured that the 
923 
state inputs to the model were within the distribution observed during training, and thus prevented 
924 
the network activity from behaving unpredictably. For all analyses, we excluded the .2 seconds 
925 
preceding or following initialization or termination frames. 
926 
 
927 
As this rollout process is serial and limited by the speed of the physical simulation, evaluating long 
928 
sessions is time consuming. To improve inference speed, we divided all recordings into 50 s 
929 
chunks and evaluated models on each chunk in parallel, using 1 CPU core per chunk.

Encoding analyses  
957 
 
958 
Feature set: We used Poisson generalized linear models (GLMs) with a log link function 70 to 
959 
predict the spiking of putative single units in DLS and MC from measurable movement features, 
960 
features inferred from physical simulation in MuJoCo, and the activations of ANN inverse 
961 
controllers. The measurable features included aligned 3D keypoint positions and velocities and 
962 
joint angular positions and velocities, spanning the entire body. Dynamic features inferred from 
963 
MuJoCo included the forces experienced at each joint, and accelerometer, velocimeter, and touch 
964 
sensors. Finally, the ANN activations included the activations of every layer of the inverse 
965 
dynamics models, considered independently.  
966 
 
967 
To ensure that the models were trained to predict movement-related activity rather than activity 
968 
during sleeping or resting, we focused only on frames in which animals were moving. To estimate 
969 
moving frames, we smoothed the keypoint velocities estimated via finite differences with a 5 frame 
970 
median filter and identified frames in which the average smoothed keypoint velocity was above a 
971 
threshold of .07 mm/frame. We then estimated sleeping frames as the set of frames resulting from 
972 
the application of 20 iterations of binary closing (binary erosion followed by binary dilation) and 
973 
500 iterations of binary opening (binary dilation followed by binary erosion) to the vector of non-
974 
moving frames. 
975 
 
976 
For all features, we used data from a temporal window containing the five surrounding samples to 
977 
predict the number of spikes in a given bin. In general, increasing the window size improved model 
978

--- PAGE 24 ---

 
predictivity up until five frames. We also trained models over a range of offsets (-1000 ms to 300 
979 
ms in 100 ms intervals) that shifted the temporal relationship of neural activity relative to each 
980 
feature.  
981 
 
982 
Regularization: As many of the features are high-dimensional, we took several steps to counter 
983 
overfitting. First, we used principal components analysis to decrease the effective dimensionality 
984 
of our feature sets, retaining only the components required to explain 90% of the variance in the 
985 
temporal windows for each feature. To further address overfitting, we used elastic net 
986 
regularization with an L1 weight of .5 and an  value of .01. Qualitatively, results were not 
987 
sensitive to changes in these parameter choices.

989 
Cross validation: We trained GLMs using a five-fold cross validation scheme. We first divided 
990 
the spiking, movement, and ANN data spanning the duration of a unit’s recording into 4-second 
991 
chunks which were randomly distributed into ten folds. We trained GLMs using training data from 
992 
nine of the folds and evaluated their performance on testing data from the remaining fold, training 
993 
a single model for each combination of training and testing sets. We use the cross-validated log-
994 
likelihood ratio (CV-LLR) and deviance-ratio pseudo-𝑅2 to quantify model predictivity, the 
995 
performance of a model in predicting spike counts in the testing set. 
996

998

999

1000

1001

Representational similarity analysis  
1004 
 
1005 
We used representational similarity analysis (RSA) to compare the representational structure of 
1006 
neural activity in DLS and MC across behaviors to measurable features of movement, dynamic 
1007 
features inferred from physical simulation, and the activations of ANN inverse controllers. RSA 
1008 
consists of three broad steps: feature vector estimation, representational dissimilarity matrix 
1009 
(RDM) estimation, and RDM comparison.  
1010 
 
1011 
Feature vector estimation: We first applied principal components analysis to each feature, 
1012 
retaining only the components required to explain 95% of the total variance. For each session, we 
1013 
used the behavioral labels from our automated behavioral segmentation, applied a 200 ms iterative 
1014 
mode filter to mitigate short-duration bouts, and divided samples from each feature into behavioral 
1015 
categories. To eventually achieve an unbiased estimate in the dissimilarity between behavioral 
1016 
categories for a given feature, we divided data into two partitions for each behavior, with odd 
1017 
instances of the behavior comprising the first partition and even instances of the behavior 
1018 
comprising the second partition. For each partition, we computed the average feature vector across 
1019 
all samples.  
1020 
 
1021 
We excluded frames in which the animal was sleeping and frames in the 40 samples surrounding 
1022 
the initiation or termination of the model’s imitation episodes. We only included sessions in which 
1023 
a minimum of 10 simultaneously recorded neurons were present throughout the entire duration of

1028

1029

1032

1033

1034

1041 
 
1042 
Estimating instantaneous motor variability: We modified the normal inference procedure to 
1043 
estimate the instantaneous motor variability of the model at each timestep. Rather than disabling 
1044 
the latent variability, we generated 50 latent samples from the latent distribution at each frame. We 
1045 
then evaluated the decoder for each sample to estimate the distribution of actions that emerged 
1046 
from a given latent distribution. We use the standard deviation across the distribution of actions 
1047 
for each actuator as the instantaneous estimate of actuator variability. 
1048 
 
1049 
To assess the significance of the predictivity of action variability and latent variability on behavior, 
1050 
we performed a permutation test. For each of 1000 iterations, we trained a logistic regression 
1051 
classifier using balanced class weights to predict the behavioral category from the vector of action 
1052 
standard deviations or latent standard deviations at each timepoint. We also trained another logistic 
1053 
regression classifier using randomly permuted category labels. The performance of both classifiers 
1054 
were evaluated with 5-fold cross validation, using the class-balanced accuracy as a performance 
1055 
metric.  
1056 
 
1057 
Variability perturbations: We further modified the inference procedure to perturb the structure of 
1058 
latent variability. Our perturbation involved varying the structure of the latent variability and 
1059 
clamping the total variability of the latent space at each timepoint. We considered three different 
1060 
structures for the latent variability. The first was a standard variability structure, in which no 
1061 
changes were made to the latent distribution. The second was a uniform variability structure in 
1062 
which each dimension of the latent space was set to equal variance for every frame. The third was 
1063 
an inverted variability structure that was constructed as follows. In each frame the latent 
1064 
dimensions were ranked according to their latent standard deviation. The standard deviations were 
1065 
then reassigned in inverse rank order such that the dimensions with the highest variability were 
1066 
assigned low variability and vice versa. To clamp the variability at a particular noise level, we 
1067 
multiplied the transformed latent variability vector by a scalar value such that the total variability 
1068 
across all dimensions in the latent space equaled the desired noise level for every frame of the 
1069 
simulation.

Data collection
Multi-camera video was acquired using Campy (https://github.com/ksseverson57/campy). Camera calibration was performed using the 
MATLAB Camera Calibration App (Mathworks) and opencv-python. Electrophysiological data was acquired using RHD2000 interface (Intan).
Data analysis
We estimated the 3D pose from multi-camera videos using DANNCE (version 1.3) (Dunn et al. 2021).  
 
Skeletal registration was performed using a custom implementation of simultaneous tracking and calibration (STAC) (Wu et al. 2013).  
 
Inverse dynamics model training was performed using CoMic (Hasenclever et al. 2020).  
 
Inverse dynamic model inference was performed using a custom python package, npmp_embedding, which depends on dm_control, 
tensorflow (version 2.3), and MuJoCo (version 2.10).  
 
Behavioral classification was performed using a custom MATLAB implementation of motion-mapper (Berman et al. 2014).  
 
Spike sorting was performed using Ephys2, a modification of FAST (Dhawale et al. 2017).  
 
All data analyses were performed with custom code written in Python using packages from the PyData stack (NumPy, SciPy, StatsModels, Sci-
kit learn, matplotlib), as well as rsatoolbox 3.0 for representational similarity analyses. Alternative control models were trained using custom 
python code dependent on Tensorflow 2.8.

Sample size
No statistical methods were used to pre-determine the number of subjects in our study but our sample sizes for electrophysiological data are 
similar to those reported in previous publications (Jin et al., 2014; Panigrahi et al., 2015; Rueda-Orozco and Robbe, 2015; Dhawale & Wolff

Data exclusions
No animals were excluded from behavioral analyses. One of the implanted animals (motor cortex) yielded a recording in which no neurons 
could be detected, and thus this animal was excluded from electrophysiological analyses.
Replication
All recordings were performed with multiple animals per group (n=3) and yielded consistent, reproducible findings.
Randomization
Animals were assigned to groups for implantation in the striatum or motor cortex using a computer-generated random number list. This 
ensured an unbiased distribution and balanced potential covariates like litter order, without directly controlling for each covariate. 
Blinding
Blinding was not feasible in our study because individual rats could be easily identified by distinctive features such as fur pattern, size, and 
behavior. Given personnel constraints, the same experimenter conducted most of the surgical implantations and behavioral experiments, 
making it impossible to blind them to the implantation sites. To minimize bias, we employed standardized and automated procedures for data 
collection and analysis where applicable.
